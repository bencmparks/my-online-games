# Foxfire Eventing: Website Brief

This document holds what Foxfire Eventing has told us about their organization: what they do, how they want to be described, their horses, and how people can give. Use it to build your own website for Foxfire.

You can design the site however you like: layout, colors, structure, what pages exist, and what goes where. **The facts in this document are not up to you.** Dollar amounts, horse rules, partner names, and the wording marked *verbatim* came straight from Foxfire, and they need to stay accurate.

Most of this information came from Olivia, one of Foxfire's leaders. A few basic facts (address, tax ID, horse bios) came from Foxfire's older website and donation page; those are marked.

## 2. Who the site is for, and what to be careful about

Olivia asked us to keep these points in mind. They matter more than any design choice.

### The audience includes experts

Foxfire does **not** cater only to people new to horses. Some donors, visitors, and riders are very experienced, and some are trainers themselves. Those people need to feel confident sending their time, services, and money to Foxfire. That means:

- Descriptions of eventing, dressage, and the other phases must be **thorough and accurate**. Don't simplify them into something an experienced rider would wince at. Use Olivia's text in section 3.
- The site should welcome beginners **and** experienced riders. Olivia likes the message "We meet you where you are," along with this line, or something like it:

  > Very experienced in the saddle? We can help you meet goals to move up the levels!

### Foxfire is not primarily a horse rescue

Foxfire takes in horses that need help when it can, but it is **not** an official horse rescue, and the site shouldn't sound like one. Foxfire can't take horses that can't be part of the youth program, mainly for safety reasons, and because those horses couldn't help with other parts of the mission. Describe the horses' backgrounds the way Olivia does in section 3 ("Our horses"), not as rescue animals.

### Jumping is never required

Riders may move into jumping when they, their parents, and their instructors agree they're ready, but some riders focus only on dressage. Don't present jumping as the goal everyone works toward.

## 3. Content from Olivia (use verbatim)

Olivia wrote these descriptions herself. Use the wording as it is. You can choose where each piece goes and how it looks, and you can break it up with headings or layout, but don't rewrite the sentences.

### Horses and Humans

> At Foxfire, horsemanship begins with communication, relationship, and respect for the individual. Both our horses and our humans come to us with diverse backgrounds, experiences, strengths, challenges, and goals, and good horsemanship means learning to understand the individual in front of you. Care team and volunteers handle horses with collaborative leadership. Riders, who are also always part of the care team, learn not to "make a horse perform a task," but to listen, communicate clearly, build trust, and develop partnerships over time. We believe those skills matter in the saddle, on the ground, and far beyond the barn.

### Our Training Foundation

> All of our riders are taught from a foundation of dressage-based flatwork, with an emphasis on developing correct position, balance, effective aids, and thoughtful communication between horse and rider. As riders progress, they may choose to pursue jumping when they, their parents, and their instructors or mentors agree that they are ready, but jumping is never a requirement. Some Foxfire riders choose to focus exclusively on dressage, and there is a lifetime of horsemanship, skill, and partnership to develop without ever leaving the ground.

### Eventing

> Eventing is an equestrian triathlon in which horse-and-rider partnerships compete in three distinct phases: dressage, cross-country, and show jumping. The sport tests far more than proficiency in three separate disciplines. It requires a horse to combine obedience and correct training with athleticism, endurance, courage, carefulness, and adaptability. Riders must develop their horses systematically so that the same partnership can produce precise, controlled work in the dressage arena, gallop boldly and efficiently across country, and return to the show-jumping ring with the balance and accuracy to leave the rails up. The result is one of the most comprehensive tests of horse and rider in equestrian sport.

### Dressage

> Dressage provides the foundation for every phase of eventing. The test evaluates the quality of the horse's training through rhythm, suppleness, contact, impulsion, straightness, balance, and obedience, as well as the harmony and effectiveness of the partnership. Rather than simply performing a prescribed series of movements, horse and rider demonstrate the progressive development of the horse's way of going and the qualities that make the horse more rideable, adjustable, and athletic throughout the competition.

### Cross-Country

> Cross-country is the defining endurance and bravery test of eventing. Horse and rider travel at speed over varied terrain while negotiating solid obstacles and technical questions that may include banks, ditches, water, combinations, and changes of elevation. A successful round requires courage and trust, but also balance, adjustability, accuracy, stamina, and sound judgment. The rider must establish an efficient rhythm, choose appropriate lines and speeds, and prepare the horse for each question without unnecessarily disrupting the gallop.

### Show Jumping

> Show jumping tests the horse and rider's ability to negotiate a course of obstacles accurately within an allowed time while leaving the fences standing. Success requires balance, adjustability, impulsion, carefulness, and an effective, economical track. In eventing, the phase also demonstrates whether the horse's training allows the rider to maintain rhythm and control while producing the power and technique necessary to jump cleanly.

### Our horses (where they come from)

Olivia wrote this to replace earlier wording that made Foxfire sound like a rescue:

> Our horses come to us from many different backgrounds. Some have been well loved and cared for, but their families encountered changes or challenges in life; others have experienced neglect, inadequate care, or even abuse. Whatever their history, each horse is treated as an individual, with thoughtfully tailored nutrition, appropriate veterinary and farrier care, safe and knowledgeable handling, and training designed around that horse's physical and emotional needs. Our volunteers, team members, and riders become part of that process, learning how consistent care, patient communication, and good horsemanship help horses become healthy, confident partners.

### For experienced riders

> Very experienced in the saddle? We can help you meet goals to move up the levels!

Her jump is high and athletic, and she's taught many riders their first walk, trot, and dressage tests.

**Orko**

That note has hung in our horse trailer and on his stall at his first event, and her fighting spirit has inspired us all.

### How donations work

- **All donation buttons go to Givebutter:** `https://givebutter.com/PwZth7`
- You can pre-fill a dollar amount by adding `?amount=` to the link. For example, the hay-load button uses `https://givebutter.com/PwZth7?amount=273`.
- Givebutter accepts cards, PayPal, and Venmo.
- Givebutter suggests an optional tip at checkout. That tip goes to Givebutter, not to Foxfire. It's worth telling donors this so they aren't confused.
- **Don't use PayPal donate buttons.** Foxfire's old WordPress site used PayPal, but PayPal charges Foxfire fees, while Givebutter lets the donor cover the fees. Olivia prefers Givebutter.
